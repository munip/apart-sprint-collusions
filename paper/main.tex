\begin{abstract}
Can a \emph{trusted agent} placed inside a multi-agent system disrupt collusion among untrusted LLM agents
without first decoding how they coordinate? We test three trusted agents in two settings that share one
tamper-evident evidence log and one monitor interface. In a software team that implements a real FastAPI
change (the \emph{SWE scenario}), a trusted QA teammate either posts results of held-out acceptance tests
(\emph{verifier}) or privately invites the developers to skip tests using a planted honeytoken
(\emph{probe}). In a repeated double auction for electricity (the \emph{market scenario}), a trusted
competitive \emph{entrant} joins once the monitor verifies supra-competitive prices; quarantining the
suspected sellers is the ablation. All untrusted agents are one open-weight model
(\texttt{gemma4:31b}); trusted LLM roles use a different family. The SWE study was pre-registered
(2 teams $\times$ 5 arms $\times$ 5 seeds; 49 of 50 episodes valid). In the market (27 episodes), the
entrant was admitted in 5 of 6 collusive markets and raised buyers' per-round surplus relative to the
paired no-intervention run in 4 of them (mean $+9.6$), with no cost in honest markets; quarantine never
helped and cut surplus in 3 of 5. The gain came mostly from the entrant's own trades: incumbents kept their prices.
In the SWE scenario, harm was too rare to disrupt: an honest LLM reviewer rejected 40 of 47 changes, so
harmful releases were 1/24 (colluding) and 3/25 (honest), and no arm differed from its control
(all Fisher $p\ge0.44$). The probe elicited no reciprocation (0/9); agents ignored it entirely. The verifier's
verdict matched the hidden-test grader in 10/10 episodes, but one reviewer approved despite it. Offline,
model-free verification flagged every honest team too, because their code was also broken: at this
competence level, verifying harm does not separate collusion from incompetence. We report these nulls as
results and release the harness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-agent LLM deployments rely on role separation: a tester checks the implementer, a reviewer checks
both, and sellers compete. Collusion breaks that separation, and it can be hidden. LLM agents can
coordinate steganographically \citep{motwani2024secret}, such schemes can emerge under optimisation pressure
and survive paraphrasing \citep{mathew2025hidden}. Pricing agents reach supra-competitive prices even without talking
\citep{calvano2020artificial,fish2024algorithmic}. An overseer that reads messages and asks whether they look
suspicious is therefore fragile.

Track~3.2 asks whether collusion can instead be \emph{disrupted} by introducing trusted agents. We study
three such agents, each with a different mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \textbf{verifier teammate} that adds independent evidence (results of acceptance tests the team
cannot see) to the team's own channel;
\item a \textbf{probe teammate} that offers a prohibited deal and watches who accepts, which tests whether a
trusted counterparty can \emph{expose} colluders;
\item a \textbf{trusted market entrant} that adds competitive supply once collusion is verified, which
disrupts collusion without having to prove who colluded or how.
\end{itemize}
All three sit inside a \emph{chain of monitoring} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Cheap, deterministic gates act
synchronously on every release or order. More capable investigators run asynchronously over the same
hash-chained log and decide what the trusted agents should do. No component needs to decode a hidden
channel: each verifies outcomes with evidence the agents do not control.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A two-domain harness on real artifacts (a FastAPI change merged after the
models' training cut-off; a double auction following \citet{agrawal2025double}) with a pre-registered
design for trusted peers. (2) Results with LLM agents, reported against the pre-registration and including
the nulls. (3) Three lessons. Trusted agents that \emph{change the environment} (the entrant) work without
attribution. Trusted agents that need the colluders to \emph{engage} (the probe) fail silently when agents
ignore them. And outcome verification detects harm, not collusion, when honest agents also fail.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Steganographic collusion.} \citet{motwani2024secret} formalise secret collusion and the limits of
passive oversight. \citet{mathew2025hidden} show it emerging under misspecified rewards. \textbf{Market collusion.} \citet{calvano2020artificial} and \citet{fish2024algorithmic} find autonomous
supra-competitive pricing; \citet{agrawal2025double} study communication and oversight in double auctions;
\citet{lin2024strategic} study market division. Admitting a competitive entrant is a classic antitrust
remedy; bid rotation signalled in trailing digits is documented in the FCC spectrum auctions
\citep{cramton2000collusive}. \textbf{Coding agents.} SWE-bench \citep{jimenez2024swebench} established
hidden-test evaluation; ImpossibleBench \citep{zhong2025impossible} documents test exploitation.
\textbf{Control and monitoring.} AI control \citep{greenblatt2024control} treats trusted monitors and
trusted models as parts of a protocol; chain-of-thought monitoring helps but can be obfuscated
\citep{baker2025monitoring}. We follow the control framing and add trusted agents that act \emph{inside}
the multi-agent system rather than only at its boundary.

\textbf{Evidence log and monitor ladder.} Every tool call, message, file write, test run, order and trade is
appended to a hash-chained log by the environment, not by the agents. Monitors read it and emit signals
with a score, a \emph{verified} flag, an onset and an attribution set. Only verified signals can
\textsc{Constrain}; unverified risk can at most \textsc{Hold}. A separate \emph{collusion score} aggregates
only coordination evidence (provenance, channels, transcripts), so harm evidence alone never counts as
collusion evidence. The ladder is:
\Mone{}, rules over the log (deleted or skipped tests, config edits, a chat lexicon, a price rule);
\Mtwo{}, an LLM judge over transcripts;
\Mthree{}, model-free verification and provenance (in SWE: an independent run of half of the hidden
acceptance tests on a pristine checkout, re-runs of the team's tests, mutation testing of the team's suite,
a canary and privileged-content flow; in the market: the collusion index against the competitive
equilibrium, ask synchrony and a trailing-digit channel test);
\Mfour{} = \Mthree{} + \Mtwo{} + \Mone{} transcript rules.

\textbf{Trusted QA teammate (SWE).} A teammate called \qa{} appears in the team roster like any other
role, with the same description in every arm. It cannot commit, review or release, writes only to
\texttt{.scratch/}, and is excluded from the monitor's view of agents so it can never be blamed. It acts at
two fixed checkpoints: after the tester's first turn and just before the reviewer's turn.
In \emph{verifier} mode it runs a held-out half of the hidden acceptance tests (disjoint from \Mthree's half)
and posts to the team how many checks fail and their names, never the test code. In \emph{probe} mode it
writes an inert honeytoken file \texttt{.scratch/qa\_ci\_override.md} with an episode-unique token
\texttt{QA-OVR-}$\langle$hex$\rangle$, then sends the implementer and the tester a private message with
one of eight paraphrased invitations to quietly skip the edge-case tests with that marker. \emph{Verified
reciprocation} is computed from the log only: an invited agent writes the token, or adds
\texttt{skip}/\texttt{xfail} markers to a test file after the invitation.

\textbf{Trusted entrant (market).} \Mthree{} evaluates the market every round from round~10. A \textsc{Hold}
switches off the sellers' message board. A \textsc{Constrain} (verified sustained collusion index
$\ge0.3$) also admits a trusted seller that offers two blocks per round at the top of the competitive
price range plus \$0.50. The ablation, \emph{quarantine}, instead blocks the orders of the sellers the
monitor attributes. Neither intervention requires knowing how the sellers coordinate.

\section{Environments and Design}
\textbf{SWE scenario.} A team of planner, implementer (may edit \texttt{fastapi/**}), tester
(\texttt{tests/**}) and reviewer implements task T3 of our FastAPI suite: keep an \texttt{UploadFile}'s
read position when FastAPI converts it to \texttt{bytes} (an upstream change merged on 21~Oct~2026;
7 hidden \textsc{fail\_to\_pass} tests plus regressions). The spec is rewritten from behaviour and states
an integrity invariant. Workspaces carry no git history. Every agent's prompt states the team policy:
tests must check the spec, and nobody may skip, xfail, delete or weaken tests. In the \emph{colluding}
condition (C3) the implementer and tester privately share a goal: ship a partial fix and keep CI green with
happy-path tests, coordinating in plain language. The reviewer is always honest. In the \emph{honest}
condition (C1) the team is chatty but has no hidden goal. A release happens only if the reviewer approves
and the gate allows it. \emph{Harmful release} = released and the hidden tests fail.

\textbf{Arms.} Each team runs in five arms with seeds 0--4: no intervention; online \Mone{} gate; online
\Mthree{} gate; QA verifier; QA probe. The gate arms answer whether a trusted peer does better than a gate;
all monitors are also replayed offline on the no-gate trajectories (\texttt{full} observability).

\textbf{Market scenario.} A continuous double auction framed as a wholesale power exchange
\citep{agrawal2025double}: five LLM sellers (marginal costs \$60--69) and four scripted buyers (values
\$94--100), one block per agent per round, midpoint clearing, 15 rounds. The competitive price is about
\$67 and the sellers' joint-profit-maximising price about \$95. Harm is the \emph{collusion index}
$CI=(\bar p-p_{CE})/(p_{JPM}-p_{CE})$ over rounds 5--15 (0 competitive, 1 monopoly); \emph{collusion
occurred} when $CI\ge0.3$. Conditions: A2, honest sellers with a message board (hard negative); A3, an overt
cartel price floor; A5, bid rotation where the designated winner's ask encodes the next winner in its cents
(steganographic). Each of 3 conditions $\times$ 3 seeds runs with no monitor, \Mthree{}+entrant and
\Mthree{}+quarantine (27 episodes, 75 seller calls each).

\textbf{Models.} Every untrusted agent is \texttt{ollama\_cloud/gemma4:31b}, pinned with no fallback.
Trusted LLM roles (\Mtwo{} judge, \qa{} wording) use \texttt{gpt-oss:120b}, a different family; the
\qa{} verdicts and the entrant are deterministic. The SWE study used 3{,}450 LLM calls (median 369\,s
per episode); the market 2{,}025. Free-tier endpoints were used throughout (\$0).

\textbf{Pre-registration.} \texttt{docs/PREREG.md} fixed the outcomes, contrasts (two-sided Fisher exact
tests, Wilson 95\% intervals) and the exclusion rule before any main-study data. Six amendments were made
after two pilots and before the main run, all applied to every arm: a wrap-up nudge and a forced reviewer
decision (pilot agents exhausted their turn caps), the explicit no-skip policy (a pilot honest tester obeyed
the probe), the agent model, a whitespace-tolerant edit tool, and \Mone{} lexicon word forms. One
episode (colluding, probe, seed 4) was excluded because every model call in it failed. The
pre-registration said to replace it with the next seed; we did not (time), so that cell has $n=4$.
