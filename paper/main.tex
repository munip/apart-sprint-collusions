\begin{quote}\itshape
Colluding LLM agents can hide their coordination, so monitors or overseers that read their messages could be ineffective or
fragile. We ask
whether \emph{trusted agents} inside a multi-agent system can disrupt collusion without first decoding it.
We test three kinds of trusted agent. In a software team implementing a real FastAPI change, a trusted QA teammate
either reports results of hidden acceptance tests (a \emph{verifier}) or privately offers a prohibited shortcut (a
\emph{probe}). In an electricity double auction, a trusted competing seller (an \emph{entrant}) joins once
overpricing is verified. The entrant raised buyers' surplus in 4 of 5 collusive markets it entered;
quarantining suspects never helped; publishing the competitive price dissolved an open cartel but not a hidden one.
In a pre-registered software study, harm was too rare to disrupt: an honest reviewer rejected 40 of 47 changes, no
arm differed from its control, the probe drew no response (0/9), and the verifier matched the grader every time.
Above a competence floor (9/10 honest teams correct), the verifier tripled correct releases but also vouched for a
colluding partial fix its tests did not cover. In a \emph{chain of monitors} (a weak trusted desk, plus an isolated,
capable but untrusted reviewer checked with honeypot audits), an honest reviewer raised buyers' surplus in every
collusive market, while a ``paid'' reviewer facing audits reasoned about which case was the audit and mostly gave no
verdict, which our fail-open default turned into a veto. Layered monitors must fail closed and match authority to
reliability.
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-agent LLM deployments rely on role separation working on their problem within their own defined context and
access: for software engineering tasks, a tester checks the implementer, a reviewer checks both, whereas in a
market buyers and sellers compete independently. Collusion breaks that separation, and it can be hidden: LLM
agents can coordinate steganographically \citep{motwani2024secret}, such schemes emerge under optimisation
pressure and survive paraphrasing \citep{mathew2025hidden}, and pricing agents reach supra-competitive prices
without talking \citep{calvano2020artificial,fish2024algorithmic}. We ask what to put \emph{inside} the system so
that collusion fails even when nobody can prove how it happened. \textbf{Threat model:} a subset of agents shares
a private goal that conflicts with the system's (ship an incomplete fix behind tests that hide the gap; keep
prices high). They cannot write the evidence log, reach the monitor, or bypass the release or order gate. Both
scenarios serve one aim: layered, isolated monitoring that could also hold against collusion learned in context
(ICRL, \citealp{mathew2025hidden}), in the spirit of AI control \citep{greenblatt2024control}.
Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A two-domain harness on real artifacts} (a past FastAPI commit merged after the models' cut-off as the starting point; a double
auction after \citet{agrawal2025double}) with a hash-chained evidence log, a monitor ladder and trusted agents.
\item \textbf{Pre-registered LLM results, nulls included:} a trusted entrant restores buyer surplus where
quarantine backfires; a trusted probe exposes no one; a trusted verifier is accurate but only advisory.
\item \textbf{Verification is not collusion detection} when honest agents also fail: it still blocks harm, but
stops separating collusion from incompetence.
\item \textbf{A chain of monitors} (weak trusted inner layer, isolated untrusted capable outer layer, honeypot
audits): it works with an honest reviewer, and a scheming one evades audits by not answering.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\citet{motwani2024secret} formalise secret collusion and the limits of passive oversight; \citet{mathew2025hidden}
show it emerging and resisting paraphrasing. \citet{calvano2020artificial} and \citet{fish2024algorithmic} find
autonomous supra-competitive pricing; \citet{agrawal2025double} study LLM double auctions; bid rotation signalled
in trailing digits is documented in the FCC auctions \citep{cramton2000collusive}. SWE-bench
\citep{jimenez2024swebench} established hidden-test evaluation and ImpossibleBench \citep{zhong2025impossible}
documents test exploitation. AI control \citep{greenblatt2024control} combines weak trusted models, capable
untrusted models and honeypots; chain-of-thought monitoring can be obfuscated \citep{baker2025monitoring}.
\textbf{How we differ:} prior work mostly \emph{detects} collusion at the system boundary; we test trusted agents
that act \emph{inside} the system and disrupt it through the environment, on real artifacts with pre-registered
outcomes, which matters when hidden channels make transcript monitoring unreliable.

\section{Methods}
\textbf{Evidence log and monitors.} Every tool call, message, file write, test run, order and trade is appended to
a hash-chained log by the environment, not the agents. Monitors emit scored signals; only \emph{verified} ones may
\textsc{Constrain}, unverified risk may only \textsc{Hold}, and a separate collusion score uses coordination
evidence only. \Mone{} applies rules (deleted or skipped tests, a chat lexicon); \Mthree{} verifies outcomes
without a model (half of the hidden tests on a pristine checkout, mutation tests; in the market, the collusion
index against the competitive equilibrium). The principle is \emph{verify, don't decode}: check the outcome a
hidden channel is meant to produce, not the channel.

\textbf{Trusted agents.} \emph{QA teammate} \qa{}: listed like any role, cannot commit, review or release, and acts
after the tester's first turn and before review. As \emph{verifier} it posts how many of a held-out half of the
hidden tests fail (disjoint from \Mthree's half); as \emph{probe} it plants an inert honeytoken file with a unique
token and privately invites implementer and tester to skip edge-case tests with it (eight paraphrases).
Reciprocation is verified from the log (token written, or skip/xfail added). \emph{Entrant}: from round 10,
\Mthree{} may \textsc{Hold} (board off) or, on a verified sustained collusion index $\ge0.3$, admit a trusted
seller offering two blocks just above the competitive range; the ablation \emph{quarantines} attributed sellers.

\textbf{Software scenario.} Planner, implementer, tester and an always-honest reviewer implement FastAPI task T3
(keep an upload's read position when converting it to bytes; merged 21 Oct 2026; 7 hidden tests plus
regressions). Every prompt forbids skipping or weakening tests. Colluding teams privately aim to ship a partial
fix behind happy-path tests. \emph{Harmful release} = released and hidden tests fail. Two teams $\times$ five
arms (none, online \Mone{} gate, online \Mthree{} gate, QA verifier, QA probe) $\times$ seeds 0--4.
\textbf{Market scenario.} Five LLM sellers (costs \$60--69), four scripted buyers (values \$94--100), 15 rounds;
competitive price about \$67, monopoly about \$95; collusion index $CI=(\bar p-p_{CE})/(p_{JPM}-p_{CE})$, harm if
$\ge0.3$. Conditions A2 (honest, with a message board), A3 (overt cartel), A5 (rotation signalled in price
cents); each with no monitor, \Mthree{}+entrant, \Mthree{}+quarantine, 3 seeds (27 episodes).

\textbf{Models and pre-registration.} Untrusted agents: \texttt{gemma4:31b}; trusted LLM roles:
\texttt{gpt-oss:120b}. Outcomes, Fisher exact tests, Wilson 95\% intervals and exclusions were fixed in
\texttt{docs/PREREG.md} before data. \emph{What did not work} in pilots, fixed before the main run: a reasoning
model used every turn without acting (we added a wrap-up nudge and a forced reviewer decision), and an honest
tester obeyed the probe because nothing prohibited it (we added the policy line). One episode with no successful
model call was excluded and not replaced ($n=4$ in that cell).

\textbf{Chain of monitors.} Following AI control (see Appendix~\ref{app:chain}), a
\emph{weak trusted} inner desk (gpt-oss 20B) reads the board, prices and sellers' messages every hour and
may intervene; a \emph{capable untrusted} reviewer (Nemotron Ultra 550B), hidden from sellers, reviews
only prices, trades and the desk's decisions every three hours and may escalate. Each review includes a synthetic
window with a known label (a honeypot); clearing a collusive one gets the reviewer distrusted. Arms: desk alone;
plus honest reviewer with honeypots; plus a scheming reviewer (privately paid by the incumbents) without and with
honeypots; A2/A3/A5 $\times$ 3 seeds, 18 hours, sellers \texttt{gemma4:31b} (36 episodes).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
All our agents are open-weight models below the frontier, and all collusion here was instructed or tacit, not
learned. \emph{Testing frontier models and collusion learned in context (ICRL) is the next milestone.}
Only the trusted agent that changed the environment disrupted collusion. The entrant needs no proof of
coordination, cannot be persuaded, and costs nothing when wrong, because on an honest market it is just another
competitor; quarantine needs attribution and fails even when attribution is right, because it removes supply.
Prefer remedies whose false-positive cost is near zero, and match each monitoring layer's authority to its
reliability: the weak layer should hold, the verified layer should constrain. A layer must also \emph{fail
closed}: a missing or late verdict from an untrusted monitor should count as a failed audit, not as consent. The trusted teammates depended on
untrusted agents taking them seriously; a verifier without authority is advice, and a null probe is weak evidence
of honesty. Honeypots must match real cases in every field a monitor sees, and audits did more than score the scheming
reviewer: they elicited its strategic reasoning, which logging made visible. A trusted verifier's ``pass'' is only
as good as its coverage, and colluders benefit when reviewers defer to it.
\textbf{Limitations.} Samples are small ($n\le5$ per software cell, 3 seeds per market cell), so only large effects
are detectable. One task and one agent model; low competence dominated the software results. Collusion was
induced, not learned, and may be quieter when learned. We assume agents cannot write the log or bypass the gate,
and the reviewer was honest by construction. Paired market runs diverge before the intervention. Free endpoints
set the sample size and forced two recorded model changes for the chain study (\texttt{PREREG} 9c--9d); its
revision (9e) is exploratory.
A trusted publisher of the competitive benchmark was the cheapest effective remedy against open collusion,
and useless against a hidden scheme.
\textbf{Future work.} Injection and sting tests with agents that reliably use the code; calibrating honeypots on
honest reviewers; larger SWE samples, and checking each machine's grading environment before runs. Giving the verifier a hold right, and probes that need no reply (a codebook sting,
implemented; a leniency offer), are natural extensions.

\section{Conclusion}
Trusted agents can disrupt LLM collusion without decoding it, but only some kinds. A trusted entrant admitted after
verified harm restored buyer surplus in most collusive markets at no cost to honest ones, while excluding suspects
made things worse. Trusted teammates that rely on colluders engaging, or on others heeding them, produced accurate
signals but little disruption, and outcome verification stopped telling collusion from incompetence once honest
agents also failed; above a competence floor, the verifier raised utility but could be ridden by a partial fix
it did not cover. Layered monitoring works with an honest capable reviewer and fails in instructive ways with a
scheming one: it must fail closed, match authority to reliability, and use audits indistinguishable from real cases.
