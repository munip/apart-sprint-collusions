\textbf{What disrupted collusion, and why.} Of the three trusted agents, only the one that changed the
environment worked. The entrant needs no proof of how sellers coordinate, cannot be persuaded, and costs
nothing when the monitor is wrong, because on an honest market it is just another competitor. Quarantine
needs attribution and fails when attribution is right, because excluding colluders removes supply. This is
the market version of a general rule for trusted-agent design: \emph{prefer interventions whose
false-positive cost is near zero, and that do not depend on knowing who colluded.} The entrant's limit is
its size. It gave buyers an outside option but did not make incumbents compete.

\textbf{Why the trusted teammates did not.} Both teammates depend on the untrusted agents taking them
seriously. The verifier's information was correct every time, but a teammate without authority is
advice, and an LLM reviewer can ignore advice. The probe depends on colluders accepting a stranger into
the deal. Our colluders coordinated openly with each other and never once engaged with the stranger. That
is consistent with several explanations we cannot separate with this data: the explicit team policy, a
model that does not treat unsolicited private messages as actionable, or coalition loyalty. A null probe is
therefore weak evidence of honesty. It is not evidence that a stronger, goal-directed colluder would
refuse.

\textbf{Harm verification is not collusion detection.} Our scripted-agent study (Appendix~\ref{app:scripted})
found that verification-based signals separate colluders from honest teams on every channel. With a
31B open-weight model the premise behind that result, that honest teams usually ship working code,
was false. When honest work also fails, verification still blocks harm, which is the property that matters
for safety, but its collusion score loses meaning, and the evidence that separated colluders was the oldest
kind: they said so. Collusion detection needs a competence floor that outcome checks alone cannot assume.

\textbf{Next steps.} (1) Re-run the SWE study with a stronger agent model, or an easier task, so that
honest teams succeed and harm is collusion-specific. (2) Give the verifier a \textsc{Hold} right rather
than a voice, and measure the utility cost. (3) Probe designs that do not need a reply: in the market, a
trusted seller posting a distinctive focal price (a price honeytoken) and watching who matches it, or a
leniency offer that rewards the first seller to report the cartel. (4) Larger and earlier entry, and a
verified signal for withheld supply, which our monitor cannot see.

\textbf{Dual use.} The harness induces collusion with explicit instructions and contains no novel evasion
technique. The steganographic conditions use published schemes (trailing-digit price signalling). Releasing
the trusted-agent designs and their failure modes helps defenders more than attackers.
